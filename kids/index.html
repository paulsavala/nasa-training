Air Detectives

The air around you is full of things too tiny to see. Scientists check on it from space, from rooftops, and even from airplanes. This book shows you how they do it, and lets you try it yourself.

Meet Tempo. Tempo is a real NASA instrument that sits on a satellite high above North America. It watches our air every hour of the day. It doesn't really have a face, but we gave it one.

Chapter one

The air is full of tiny things

Air looks empty. It isn't! Every breath you take has billions and billions of tiny bits floating in it. Some are bits of dust blown off dry ground. Some are specks of smoke from fires and chimneys. And some are gas molecules, like nitrogen dioxide (scientists write it NO₂), which comes out of car and truck engines and power plants.

How tiny are they? Let's compare them with one of your hairs. Slide the zoom and keep going.

PM2.5 means “particles 2.5 micrometers across or smaller.” A micrometer (µm) is one-thousandth of a millimeter. About 30 PM2.5 specks lined up would be as wide as one hair. They are so small they can sneak deep into your lungs, so scientists keep a close eye on them.
🔬

NO₂ molecules are too small to see even with the strongest zoom here. About 1,000 of them in a row would only be as wide as one smoke speck.

Chapter two

Sunlight bounces back to space

Satellites can't scoop up air to test it. They are much too far away. Instead, they use sunlight.

Sunlight looks white, but it is really a mix of colors: red, green, blue, and more. It also has a color our eyes can't see at all, called ultraviolet or UV. (Bees can see it!) In the picture, we drew UV light purple so you can see it.

When sunlight hits something, some colors bounce off and some get soaked up. Tempo catches the light that bounces back up and measures how much of each color came back. Pick something for the light to hit and watch.

💡

Light is super fast. It takes about 8 minutes to travel from the Sun to Earth, and only about one-eighth of a second to bounce from the ground up to Tempo.

Why is the sky blue? Air itself bounces a little light around, and it bounces blue and UV light much more than red. That scattered blue light comes at your eyes from every part of the sky. That's why you see a little blue and UV even over plain grass in the picture.

Chapter three

The zoomer and the hoverer

Scientists use two kinds of satellites to watch the air.

The zoomer flies low, about 700 km up. It races all the way around Earth in about 100 minutes, which is about 14 laps every day! Since Earth turns underneath it, it sees a new strip each lap. But it only passes over your house about once a day.

The hoverer flies far out, 35,786 km up. Out there, one lap takes exactly one day, the same time Earth takes to spin once. So it seems to hang still over the same spot. Tempo rides on a hoverer, so it can look at North America again and again, every hour.

The orange dots show where the zoomer has been. The yellow beam shows Tempo looking at North America.
🏀

If Earth were a basketball, the zoomer would fly only about 1 cm above it, about the thickness of your finger. Tempo would be about 67 cm away, as far as from your shoulder to your fingertips!

🌙

Tempo needs sunlight to work, so it takes a rest at night, when it's dark over North America.

Chapter four

A picture made of squares

Tempo doesn't snap a whole picture at once. It looks at one thin stripe, then the next, sweeping from east to west across the whole country. It takes about an hour, and then it starts again.

Each little square in the picture is called a pixel. One Tempo pixel covers about 2 km by 5 km of ground. Walking across one the long way would take you more than an hour! Pick a time of day and press Scan.

Rush hour is when lots of people drive to work or home at the same time. More cars means more NO₂ near the roads. On hot, sunny days, sunlight can “cook” NO₂ together with other gases into ozone, which makes it harder to breathe.

Chapter five

Be the detective: cloud, smoke or dust?

Here is a real puzzle scientists solve every day. Tempo sends back how much of each color bounced up from one pixel. Your job is to figure out what was there. Use the clues!

Clue cards
☁️ Cloud: every bar is tall, even UV. Clouds are bright and white.
🔥 Smoke: UV is tiny, and the other three bars are about the same height. Smoke is gray and soaks up UV.
🏜️ Dust: the bars go up like stairs, with red the tallest. Dust is yellow-brown.
🌱 Clean air: all bars low, with green a bit taller.
Look at the four bars. Which clue card matches?

The real smoke and dust detector uses the same kinds of clues: how bright a pixel is, how much UV it soaks up, and how its colors compare. It also uses a second satellite that sees heat, to tell clouds and dust apart better.

Chapter six

A robot that stares at the Sun

How do scientists know Tempo is getting the right answer? They check it from the ground! On rooftops all over the world sit instruments called Pandoras. A Pandora is a little robot with a telescope that follows the Sun all day long.

Sunlight has to pass through all the air above the rooftop to reach the Pandora. Gases like NO₂ soak up a tiny bit of certain colors on the way, so Pandora can count how much NO₂ is up there. It measures every few minutes. Tempo measures once an hour. Slide through the day and see if they agree.

🌍

There are Pandoras in more than 100 places around the world. Their measurements go to one big shared collection that any scientist can use. Even kids!

Chapter seven

What do air scientists actually do?

Most of a scientist's day isn't spent with rockets. It's spent asking good questions and being careful with numbers. Here's how a real project goes, in order:

  1. Ask a question

    “Was our air worse on the day smoke blew in from faraway fires?”

  2. Collect data

    Download Tempo's pictures and Pandora's measurements for that day.

  3. Clean it up

    Throw out pixels covered by clouds and measurements marked “not sure.”

  4. Make maps and graphs

    Turn thousands of numbers into pictures people can understand.

  5. Compare

    Do the satellite and the rooftop robot agree? If not, why not?

  6. Share and ask again

    Tell other scientists what you found. Every answer leads to a new question!

Scientists use computers for the “clean it up” step, because there are millions of pixels. They write instructions called code. Here's a tiny bit of real-looking code. Can you read what it does?

# keep only the clear pixels
for pixel in picture:
    if pixel.cloud > 0.3:
        throw_away(pixel)
    else:
        keep(pixel)

It says: look at every pixel. If more than 3 tenths of it is cloud, throw it away. Otherwise, keep it.

Chapter eight

Try it: make a sunset in a jar

Tiny bits in the air bounce blue light around more than red light. You can see this yourself with a glass of water and a few drops of milk. The milk is full of tiny bits too.

You need

A tall clear glass or jar, water, milk, a spoon, a flashlight, and a dark room. Ask a grown-up to help.

What to do

  1. Fill the glass with water.
  2. Shine the flashlight through the side. Look at the water from the side and through the far end.
  3. Stir in a few drops of milk and look again.
  4. Add more drops, a few at a time. What happens to the colors?
🌇

At sunset, sunlight goes sideways through lots and lots of air before it reaches you. Most of the blue gets bounced away on the trip. The light that's left looks orange and red, just like the end of your jar. Smoky or dusty air makes sunsets even redder.

Be a haze watcher

Pick something far away you can see from your window: a hill, a tower or a tall tree. Every day, give it a score from 1 (crystal clear) to 5 (can't see it). Write down the date and your score. After a few weeks you'll have your own data, just like a real scientist!

For grown-ups

Everything here is simplified from real science, and the pictures are toy versions of real instruments. The orbit heights, the lap times, the 2 km × 5 km pixel size and the hourly east-to-west scan are real. TEMPO (Tropospheric Emissions: Monitoring of Pollution) launched in 2023. It sits on a commercial satellite above the equator at 91° W. Pandora instruments belong to the Pandonia Global Network.
